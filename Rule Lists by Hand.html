Rule lists by hand

The catapult model learns rule lists from experiments. This page rebuilds it from the smallest possible pieces, with one or two features, so every number can be checked by hand. The outcomes are the ball's three behaviours:

Each step adds one idea. The data in steps 2 and 3 is the toy example from the report: tool drops at −150 and −60 px from the pivot (ball stationary) and at +40 and +90 px (ball catapulted).

Step 1 of 4

One bucket: counting with pretend counts

Forget rules for a moment. Put every experiment into one bucket and ask: how likely was each outcome, given only the experiments before it? Every outcome starts with a pretend count of 0.5, so nothing is ever impossible:

chance of next outcome k = (times k seen so far + 0.5) / (experiments so far + 1.5)

Multiply those chances over all experiments and you get the bucket's likelihood: how well "one bucket" explains the data. A bucket full of one outcome scores high; a mixed bucket scores low.

likelihood of this bucket (product of the last column)On its own this number means little: it shrinks with every experiment you add. It is only ever compared with other candidates' scores on the same data, which is what step 2 does.
prediction for the next experiment

Try this

  • Reset, then add four stationary: each one is less surprising than the last (chances 0.33, 0.60, 0.71, 0.78), and the likelihood ends at 0.11.
  • Now add one catapult: its chance is only 0.5/5.5 = 0.09, and the likelihood drops by that factor.
  • Pure beats mixed: two experiments (stat, stat) score 0.33 × 0.60 = 0.20, while (stat, cat) score 0.33 × 0.20 = 0.067. Bigger is better.

Why pure buckets score higher. The likelihood is "how well did the bucket see each outcome coming?" In a pure bucket every new experiment repeats what the bucket has already seen, so each chance is larger than the last (0.33, 0.60, 0.71, 0.78…). In a mixed bucket every switch to a different outcome is a surprise and gets a small chance (0.14, 0.09…), and those small factors drag the product down. So a rule that sorts experiments into pure buckets explains them better than one mixed bucket. One caution: every factor is below 1, so a bucket's score also shrinks as it holds more experiments. Only compare candidates that explain the same full set of experiments, as step 2 does.

  • The default data (stat, stat, cat, cat) gives 1/3 × 3/5 × 1/7 × 1/3 = 1/105 ≈ 0.0095: the "no rule" number in the report.
Step 2 of 4

One rule on one feature: the rule only sorts, the buckets predict

Now there is one feature, x = tool.x − pivot.x, and at most one rule: IF x < t (or x > t), with the rest going to the default bucket. The rule has no outcome of its own. It only decides which experiments go into which bucket; each bucket then predicts from its own counts, exactly as in step 1.

The model's question is which rule to believe. Every candidate gets prior × likelihood. The prior gives "no rule" 1/3 and "one rule" 2/3, shared evenly over both directions and every cutoff from −200 to 200. Divide by the total and you have the posterior.

Step 3 of 4

Search instead of listing: Metropolis–Hastings

Step 2 listed all 801 candidates. The real model has far too many to list, so it wanders: propose a small edit, compute how much better or worse it is, keep it with that probability. Run it on the step-2 data and watch the visit frequencies converge to the exact answer from step 2.

Edits used here. From "no rule": add a random rule. From a rule: remove it (half the time), or re-pick the cutoff exactly in proportion to likelihood (the other half; always kept). The add/remove decision uses

accept if random < (prior ratio) × (likelihood ratio) × (Hastings correction)

The Hastings correction is (chance of proposing the reverse edit) / (chance of proposing this edit). From "no rule" the only edit is ADD (chance 1); from a rule, REMOVE is proposed half the time (chance 1/2). So ADD gets 1/2 ÷ 1 = 0.5 and REMOVE gets 1 ÷ 1/2 = 2. Without it, rules would be entered more easily than left, and the chain would spend too little time at "no rule".

Worked example on the toy data

Scores from step 2: one mixed bucket ("no rule") has likelihood 1/3 × 3/5 × 1/7 × 1/3 = 1/105. A rule with its cutoff in the −60…+40 gap has (1/3 × 3/5) × (1/3 × 3/5) = 1/25. Prior ratio of "one rule" to "no rule" = (2/3) ÷ (1/3) = 2. (Each rule's prior is the 2/3 for "one rule" shared over 2 directions × 400 cutoffs = 800 rules: (2/3) × (1/2) × (1/400) = (2/3) × 1/800 = 1/1200. ADD proposes a specific rule with chance 1/800 too, so the 1/800 cancels and only the 2 remains.)

Both likelihoods multiply four chances, one per experiment. They differ because each experiment is predicted only by the earlier experiments in its own bucket:

The catapults are the difference: in the mixed bucket they follow two stationary experiments and are a surprise (1/7); in their own bucket they start fresh (1/3) and then repeat (3/5). Ratio: (1/25) ÷ (1/105) = 105/25 = 4.2.

So the chain jumps into gap rules easily and leaves them reluctantly (24% of REMOVE proposals). That is how it ends up spending more time on the rules that explain the data better.

Step 4 of 4

Two features and AND: why conditions combine

Add a second feature, h = drop height above the arm. A rule may now test one feature or both joined by AND. The prior prefers one condition (2/3) over two (1/3), so the model only uses AND when the data needs it. Cutoffs are on a 10 px grid here so all 6,561 candidates can be listed exactly.
